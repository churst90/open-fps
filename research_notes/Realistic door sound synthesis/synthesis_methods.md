# Synthesis methods for realistic impact and contact sounds of complex objects (doors): why pure modal synthesis sounds tonal, and how published work gets realism

Scope note: these notes cover published synthesis methods and the numbers behind them. Door-specific mechanics (latches, closers, seals, air bind) are only touched where a synthesis paper deals with them. Worked numbers for door panels are marked as inferences and show how they were computed.

---

## 1. Modal synthesis plus stochastic/residual components (the method families)

### Takeaway
Every published system that gets close to recordings adds something non-modal to the damped-sinusoid bank. Options include a residual taken from a recording (Serra/SMS; Lloyd 2011; Ren 2013), separate transient grains (Picard 2009), a physically computed rigid-body "acceleration noise" click (Chadwick 2012), nonlinear coupling between modes (Harmonic Shells 2009), filtered noise (Aramaki 2006; Cook PhISEM), or micro-collision trains (van den Doel 2001). Ren et al. state it directly: "linear modal synthesis alone is not capable of synthesizing sounds that are as rich and realistic as many real-world recordings."

### Cited Findings

**Serra & Smith spectral modelling (SMS), the root of "deterministic + stochastic"**
- SMS splits a sound into stable sinusoids (partials) and a noisy residual. Spectral peaks that change slowly across STFT frames are joined into partials, the partials are subtracted, and "a coarse spectral envelope is computed from the residual, which is used to shape white noise in the synthesis step." — [Lloyd, Raghuvanshi & Govindaraju 2011, "Sound synthesis for impact sounds in video games", ACM I3D](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf), describing [Serra & Smith 1990, Computer Music Journal]
- The deterministic+stochastic model "was introduced to spectral synthesis by Serra and Smith [1990]… a deterministic part, which are time-variant sinusoids, and a stochastic part, which is obtained by spectral subtraction of the deterministic sinusoids from the original audio." Cook (1996, 1997, 2002) and Lloyd et al. (2011) modify both parts at resynthesis. — [Ren, Yeh & Lin 2013, "Example-guided physically based modal sound synthesis", ACM TOG 32(1)](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf) ([ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/2421636.2421637))

**Ren, Yeh & Lin 2013: example-guided modal synthesis with residual compensation**
- Model: FEM (tetrahedral) with Rayleigh damping C = αM + βK. The decoupled system q̈ + (αI + βΛ)q̇ + Λq = Uᵀf gives modes qᵢ = aᵢ e^(−dᵢt) sin(2πfᵢt + θᵢ), with dᵢ = ½(α + βλᵢ) and fᵢ = (1/2π)·sqrt(λᵢ − ((α+βλᵢ)/2)²). — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- A structural limitation of Rayleigh damping: all modes lie on one circle in (ω, d) space, ω² + (d − 1/β)² = (1/β²)(1 − αβ). "This constraint for modes restricts the model from capturing some sound effects and renders it impossible to make modal synthesis sounds with Rayleigh damping exactly the same as an arbitrary real-world recording." — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Why a residual is needed, in the authors' words: "during the short period of contact, not all energy is transformed into stable vibration that can be represented with a small number of damped sinusoids, or modes. The stochastic and transient nature of the non-modal components makes sounds in nature rich and varying." — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Feature extraction: multi-level power spectrograms, each level doubling frequency resolution and halving time resolution. Peaks are found coarse to fine (the coarsest frequency bin can be 86 Hz), then a damped-sinusoid shape is fitted locally to each peak. On 100 synthetic sinusoids with f in [0, 22050] Hz and d in [0.1, 1000] s⁻¹, average relative error was 0.040 % for frequency and 0.53 % for damping. — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Residual computation: the modes come from estimated material parameters, not from tracking, so they do not line up with the recording and direct subtraction fails. The method builds a "represented sound": each extracted reference feature is weighted by its match ratio to the estimated modes. Residual power spectrogram = recorded − represented. — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Residual transfer to a new object or hit point. Per source→target mode pair: (1) shift the residual spectrogram in frequency from fᵢˢ to fⱼᵗ; (2) stretch it in time by the damping ratio dᵢˢ/dⱼᵗ; (3) scale intensity pixel by pixel to match the target modal spectrogram. Each region runs from ½(f_{i−1}+f_i) to ½(f_i+f_{i+1}), and the regions are stitched together. Time signal: Griffin–Lim iterative inverse STFT, 10 iterations was "acceptable". This ran in real time on a GTX 480 at about 1 contact per 1/30 s. When shapes differ, the top N_dominant modes on each side are paired from low frequency to high. — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Estimated Rayleigh parameters (α, β): Wood 2.1364, 3.0828e‑6; Plastic 52.627, 8.7753e‑7; Metal 6.3035, 2.1160e‑8; Glass 18.301, 1.4342e‑7; Porcelain 0.037388, 8.4142e‑8. — [Ren et al. 2013, Table I](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Material-recognition study, 53 subjects. Recorded wood was identified as wood 50.7 % and as plastic 47.9 %, so wood and plastic are confusable even in real recordings. Synthesized metal was identified as metal 69.6 %. — [Ren et al. 2013, Tables III–IV](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)

**Lloyd, Raghuvanshi & Govindaraju 2011 (shipped in Crackdown 2, Xbox 360)**
- "We show that for many sounds greater quality is obtained by using the amplitude envelopes of the extracted modes directly rather than fitting the envelopes to the standard exponential decay model. When combined with a residual, the synthesized sounds in most cases are indistinguishable from recorded clips." — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)
- Residual = original minus synthesized modal part (as in SMS). "For sounds with a strong modal component, the energy in the residual is mostly noise concentrated near the attack. The tail is empty and can be clipped." Letting "the short duration, high frequency modes fall into the residual keeps the residual short while maintaining fidelity." There is a trade-off: modes moved into the residual stop varying, and a residual that is too large "can drown out the variation". — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)
- Mode counts kept after analysis (modal size, mode count, residual size): Brass bell 492 B / 6 modes / 65.2 KB; Plastic barrel 998 B / 23 modes / 28.7 KB; Wooden box 2381 B / 65 modes / 21.1 KB; Rock 4064 B / 120 modes / 11.9 KB. The broadband "Rock" needed the most modes and was the case the noise-filter variation method suited best. — [Lloyd et al. 2011, Fig. 5](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)
- Envelope storage: log-amplitude, values clamped below a noise floor (e.g. −81 dB), greedy simplification (3–4× fewer samples), linear interpolation in log space. Samples "tend to cluster near the initial attack of the sound where most of the complexity lies". Amplitudes quantised to 8 bits with "very little or no perceptual difference". — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)

**Picard, Tsingos & Faure 2009: retargeting example sounds (grains)**
- Recordings are segmented automatically into atomic grains. Continuous-contact recordings are split into sinusoidal and transient components that are encoded separately. A method similar to matching pursuit represents each recording as a compact series of grains, which are triggered from physics-engine parameters. — [Picard, Tsingos & Faure 2009, AES 35th Int. Conf. Audio for Games (HAL inria-00394469)](https://hal.science/inria-00394469)
- Details from the thesis. Grains are "typically 300 to 3000 samples long, i.e. 0.01 to 0.1 s @ 44.1 kHz". Recordings are cut at inflection points of spectral flux (Euclidean distance between normalised spectra of consecutive frames). Each grain gets a Tukey window with taper r = 0.02, power-normalised, and low-energy grains are discarded. Grains are labelled transient or continuous. Each recording is re-encoded by greedy cross-correlation peak picking against the grain dictionary. Resynthesis concatenates grains with overlap-add, searching within half the median grain length, at "an amplitude equal to the power of the original audio recording in the considered time-window normalized by the power of the grain". Impulsive grains are triggered at penetration-force peaks. — [Picard PhD thesis 2009, Univ. Nice / INRIA, ch. 9](http://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2009/Pic09/Thesis_CPicard_AftDS.pdf)
- On contact time, from the thesis: "a short contact time creates an impulse-like transient with a rich spectrum whereas a long contact time produces a damped transient where the high frequency content is narrow." — [Picard thesis 2009](http://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2009/Pic09/Thesis_CPicard_AftDS.pdf)
- Picard & Faure's multi-scale modal analysis: a coarse 2×2×2 hexahedral FEM gave 81 modes (75 used), against 822 modes (816 non-rigid) for a full tetrahedral mesh. Rayleigh parameters were set to 3×10⁻⁷ (stiffness) and 10 (mass), and "the use of a constant damping ratio is a simplification". — [Picard & Faure, "A robust and multi-scale modal analysis for sound synthesis", DAFx-09](https://www.dafx.de/paper-archive/2009/papers/paper_47.pdf)

**van den Doel, Kry & Pai 2001 (FoleyAutomatic)**
- Modal model M = {f, d, A}: y_k(t) = Σₙ a_{nk} e^(−dₙt) sin(2πfₙt), where the gain matrix A depends on contact location. Modes are driven by an audio-rate "audio-force" (contact forces at audio rate, well above the physics rate). — [van den Doel, Kry & Pai 2001, SIGGRAPH](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)
- "Even though modal models by themselves are a useful class of models, that by itself is not sufficient. For realistic contact sounds we need good, physically-based models of the contact forces." Stochastic models are often appropriate. — [van den Doel et al. 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)

**Bonneel, Drettakis, Tsingos, Viaud-Delmon & James 2008 (frequency-domain modal)**
- Synthesizes modes in the STFT domain by exploiting the sparsity of each mode's spectrum. Keeping 3 bins per mode gives 4.7 % energy error, 5 bins gives 1.1 %, and 1 bin gives 52.7 %. — [Bonneel et al. 2008, "Fast modal sounds with scalable frequency-domain synthesis", ACM TOG 27(3)](https://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2008/BDTVJ08/FastModalSounds.pdf)
- Weak point: the Hann window and constant-exponential approximation "degrades the onset or 'attack' for high frequency modes", suppressing "the 'crispness' of the attacks". The fix is a special first frame that starts with a rectangular sub-window, followed by Hann sub-windows, at about 1.2 % extra cost. — [Bonneel et al. 2008](https://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2008/BDTVJ08/FastModalSounds.pdf)
- "Most of the energy of an impact sound is usually concentrated in a small number N of modes (typically 3)." — [Bonneel et al. 2008](https://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2008/BDTVJ08/FastModalSounds.pdf)

**Chadwick, An & James 2009 (Harmonic Shells): nonlinear mode coupling**
- Linear modes coupled by nonlinear thin-shell forces, with O(r²) cost per time step for r coupled modes. Example models: trash can, 200 modes spanning 0.071–4.43 kHz, 0.75 m, 2 mm steel; trash lid, 200 modes, 0.112–6.79 kHz; water bottle, 300 modes; recycling bin, 300 modes; cymbal, 500 modes, 0.061–9.94 kHz, Δt = 1/88200. — [Chadwick, An & James 2009, ACM TOG 28(5)](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/HarmonicShells/HarmonicShells09.pdf)
- With the nonlinear model, the trash can makes a characteristic "crashing" sound. The linear modal model makes a "ding". The paper says thin shells "produce proverbial 'crashes' and 'rumbles' that are poorly approximated by linear modal sound models which lack nonlinear mode coupling." The authors also had to limit an artificial "pitch glide" for violent impacts. — [Harmonic Shells project page](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/HarmonicShells/); [paper](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/HarmonicShells/HarmonicShells09.pdf)

**Zheng & James 2010 (fracture soundbanks)**
- A glass table setting smashed into more than 300 pieces was rendered as a superposition of 4046 modal vibrations up to 14 kHz. Ellipsoid proxies from a precomputed soundbank, indexed by inertia and scaled to match the fundamental, gave about 500× speedup. Scaling the geometry by γ gives ω → γ⁻¹ω, Rayleigh damping α + βω² → α + βγ⁻²ω², U → γ^(−3/2)U. The fracture solver steps at about 5 kHz "to resolve micro-collisions and fracture events". — [Zheng & James 2010, ACM TOG 29(4)](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/FractureSound/files/fractureSound_comp.pdf)

**Aramaki & Kronland-Martinet 2006: subtractive noise synthesis of impacts**
- Impact analysis–synthesis by "dynamic filtering of noisy input signals that simulates the damping of spectral components". Modal contributions are made additively or with banded waveguides, and band-pass filtering stands for the interaction with the exciter. Parameters are tied to perceived material and geometry. — [Aramaki & Kronland-Martinet 2006, IEEE TASLP 14(2)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/3457529_Analysis-Synthesis_of_impact_sound_by_real_time_dynamic_filtering)

**Rocchesso / Avanzini (Sounding Object project): physical contact models**
- Impact uses a lumped hammer-resonator with the Marhefka–Orin / Hunt–Crossley force f_h = k_h·x^α + λ_h·x^α·ẋ for penetration x > 0 (else 0), α = 3/2. Resonator: m_o ẍ_o + r_o ẋ_o + k_o x_o = f_h. The system is discretised with the bilinear transform s = 2F_s(1−z⁻¹)/(1+z⁻¹), and the delay-free loop is solved iteratively. The authors argue that a physical exciter keeps "complex and realistic attack transients" that damped-sinusoid signal models lose. — [Avanzini & Rocchesso 2001, ICMC](https://avanzini.di.unimi.it/downloads/publications/avanzini_icmc01_finalsub.pdf)
- Friction (used in the project's squeaky swinging door) is an elasto-plastic bristle model, f = σ₀z + σ₁ż + σ₂v + σ₃w. z is mean bristle deflection, governed by a nonlinear first-order ODE in v. σ₀ is bristle stiffness, σ₁ bristle damping, σ₂ viscous friction, and w is fractal noise for surface roughness. The ODE makes the v→f map dynamic, so it "is able to account for more realistic acoustic transients during non-stationary interaction". Applications: a rolling/braking wheel, a rubbed glass, a squeaky swinging door (book ch. 8). — [Rocchesso et al. 2004, ISon workshop](https://avanzini.di.unimi.it/downloads/publications/rocchesso_ison04_finalsub.pdf); book: [Rocchesso & Fontana (eds.) 2003, The Sounding Object](https://www.soundobject.org/SObBook/SObBook_JUL03.pdf)

**Cook: PhISEM (Physically Informed Stochastic Event Modeling)**
- PhISEM is stochastic granular synthesis driven by the statistics of particle physics. Collisions occur as a Poisson process, system energy decays exponentially, and each collision is a rapidly decaying noise burst through a few resonant filters. Instruments: maraca, sekere, cabasa, bamboo chimes, water drops, tambourine, sleighbells, guiro. — [Csound STKShakers manual](https://csound.com/docs/manual/STKShakers.html); original paper [Cook 1997, "Physically Informed Sonic Modeling (PhISM): Synthesis of percussive sounds", Computer Music Journal 21(3):38–49](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Physically-Informed-Sonic-Modeling-(PhISM):-of-Cook/91a5ea5d5aa6acf3e004c75302896d70c0ff43f5)

**Ahmad & Kondoz 2011 (dictionary/atom resynthesis)**
- Impact recordings are decomposed with the stationary wavelet transform into a K‑SVD-learned dictionary (K = 108 atoms), then resynthesized from sparse patterns plus a small random perturbation vector for non-repetition. T₀ = 7 atoms already gave "perceptually acceptable" quality, and quality rose with 14 and 21 atoms. Stimuli were short impacts (T60 < 100 ms) at 44.1 kHz. — [Ahmad & Kondoz, DAFx-11](http://recherche.ircam.fr/pub/dafx11/Papers/92_e.pdf)

### Inferences
- Across these papers the non-modal energy sits in the attack: residuals are "mostly noise concentrated near the attack" with an empty tail (Lloyd), Bonneel's error is in the first frame, and Chadwick's acceleration noise lasts only the contact pulse. The first ~5–30 ms is where a modal-only door will sound most wrong, and where noise or transient layers buy the most realism.
- Two of the methods are physical rather than data-driven: Chadwick's acceleration noise and Harmonic Shells' nonlinear coupling. Both fit a "model the thing, not the sound" constraint. The residual methods (Ren, Lloyd, Picard) all need a recording of the target object.

## 2. When and why modal-only synthesis fails: modal density, modal overlap, thresholds, damping

### Takeaway
A linear modal bank is correct when modes are sparse and well separated (modal overlap M = n·η·f < 1). Above M ≈ 1 the response is a smooth, overlapping continuum that SEA treats statistically. Synthesizing that region with a handful of lightly damped sinusoids makes isolated pitched "rings" where the real object has dense, beating, noise-like energy. A thin steel door skin has M > 1 from roughly 100 Hz upward (worked below). A solid timber leaf stays sparse until about 1–4 kHz. Exponential-envelope modes also miss real envelope shapes, and Rayleigh damping forces every mode onto one damping curve.

### Cited Findings
- Modal overlap: M_ov = n·η·f (n = modal density in modes/Hz, η = loss factor, f = Hz), "the ratio of the damping bandwidth to the average separation of the natural frequencies of the modes. It measures the 'smoothness' of the frequency response function." "Statistical energy analysis is suitable if the modal overlap is > 1. Otherwise, deterministic methods, such as the finite element or boundary element method may be performed." — [Irvine, "Statistical Energy Analysis Parameters", Rev X (vibrationdata)](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials2/SEA_parameters_revX.pdf), citing Wijker 2009
- One overview gives high-frequency (SEA-valid) thresholds of MOF = 1 for beams, 2.5 for plates and 3 for acoustic elements. — [ScienceDirect Topics, "Modal density – an overview"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/modal-density) (search-snippet level only, not read in full)
- Plate bending modal density (generic BCs): n(ω) = (S/4π)·sqrt(m/B), with B = Eh³/(12(1−ν²)) and m = mass/area. Simply supported, free and clamped versions add an edge term ∓(a+b)/(π·sqrt(ω))·(m/B)^(1/4)·(¼ or ½). n(f) = 2π·n(ω). A circular plate has the same modal density as a rectangular plate of the same area. — [Irvine SEA parameters, App. I](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials2/SEA_parameters_revX.pdf)
- Worked check from the same author: aluminium panel, 5 mm, c_L = 5100 m/s, gives 0.068 modes/Hz per m² (14.6 Hz per mode); 2 m² gives 0.137 modes/Hz. Bending phase speed is C_B ≈ sqrt(1.8·c_L·h·f) (dispersive). — [Irvine, "Modal Density" Rev C](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials_alt/mdens.pdf)
- Typical panel dissipation loss factor (Wijker, via Irvine): η = 0.05 for f ≤ 80 Hz; η = 1.8/f^0.87 for 80 Hz < f < 2500 Hz; η = 0.002 for f ≥ 2500 Hz. Built-up sandwich panel: η = 0.05 below 500 Hz, 0.05·(500/f) above. Bare sandwich: η = 0.3/f^0.63. Cylindrical shell: 0.002–0.03 below 3 kHz. — [Irvine SEA parameters, App. F](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials2/SEA_parameters_revX.pdf)
- SEA's own assumptions include "primary response is resonant", "small modal damping, equal for all modes in a given frequency band", traditionally "steady-state incoherent broadband random excitation". "Transient SEA methods have also been developed". "Boundary conditions become less relevant at higher frequencies." — [Irvine SEA parameters](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials2/SEA_parameters_revX.pdf)
- Mode counts in practice: a glass table set took 4046 modes up to 14 kHz ([Zheng & James 2010](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/FractureSound/files/fractureSound_comp.pdf)). Thin shells took 200–500 coupled modes ([Harmonic Shells](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/HarmonicShells/HarmonicShells09.pdf)). Recording-fitted game assets took 6 (bell) to 120 (rock) modes plus a residual ([Lloyd 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)). Prior progressive-modal demos used "a few thousand modes" ([Bonneel 2008](https://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2008/BDTVJ08/FastModalSounds.pdf)).
- Envelope shape matters: amplitude envelopes sampled from recordings beat ideal exponentials ([Lloyd 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)). For glass and steel "an exponential decay envelope is probably a too poor approximation" ([Avanzini & Rocchesso 2001](https://avanzini.di.unimi.it/downloads/publications/avanzini_icmc01_finalsub.pdf)).
- Rayleigh damping restriction (all modes on one circle in (ω, d)) makes an arbitrary recording unreachable — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Perceived material depends more on decay than on pitch. In Avanzini & Rocchesso's test, "decay (or quality factor q_o) plays a much larger role than pitch f_o in material perception". Ranges of q_o and decay time t_e where more than 50 % of subjects chose each material: rubber q_o 5–44.3, t_e 0.0008–0.0141 s; wood q_o 14.9–131.8, t_e 0.0023–0.0353 s; glass q_o 189.6–5000, t_e 0.0434–1.125 s; steel q_o 272.8–5000, t_e 0.0434–1.59 s. Glass and steel overlap heavily. — [Avanzini & Rocchesso 2001, Table 2](https://avanzini.di.unimi.it/downloads/publications/avanzini_icmc01_finalsub.pdf)
- Small, hard objects show the extreme case. Their modes can lie entirely above hearing: the ball bearing's lowest mode is 159.5 kHz and the dice's 32.9 kHz. "Modal sound synthesis produces no meaningful sound" for them, and the audible click is acceleration noise. For a coin (lowest mode 10.07 kHz) "modal synthesis alone sound[s] muffled and provide[s] little temporal distinction between object impacts." — [Chadwick, Zheng & James 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)
- The auditory filter bandwidth (ERB) is ERB(f) = 24.7·(4.37·f/1000 + 1) Hz: about 35 Hz at 100 Hz, 79 Hz at 500 Hz, 133 Hz at 1 kHz, 241 Hz at 2 kHz. — [Glasberg & Moore 1990, Hearing Research 47:103–138](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-5955(90)90170-T)

### Inferences (worked numbers, using the formulas above)
- Closed form: with m = ρh and B = Eh³/(12(1−ν²)), the generic plate density is n(f) = √3·S/(h·c_L), where c_L = sqrt(E/(ρ(1−ν²))). This reproduces Irvine's 0.068 modes/Hz for 5 mm aluminium at 1 m².
- **Steel door skin**, 1 mm, S ≈ 1.8 m², c_L ≈ 5400 m/s: n ≈ 0.58 modes/Hz, i.e. a mode every ~1.7 Hz. With Irvine's panel η = 1.8/f^0.87: M ≈ 1.9 at 100 Hz, 2.1 at 200 Hz, 2.3 at 500 Hz, 2.6 at 1 kHz, 2.8 at 2 kHz. **M > 1 across the audible band above roughly 50–100 Hz.** A steel skin's high-frequency response is statistical, not a few rings. Each ERB at 1 kHz contains about 75 modes. (0.8 mm skin: n ≈ 0.72.)
- **Solid timber leaf**, 40 mm, assuming c_L ≈ 3000 m/s (an assumption, wood is orthotropic): n ≈ 0.026 modes/Hz (~38 Hz spacing). M = 1 at about 3.8 kHz if η = 0.01, or about 770 Hz if η = 0.05. A solid timber leaf really is modal (sparse) through the low-mid band. A hollow-core leaf with 3 mm skins (c_L ≈ 2000 assumed) behaves like the steel case (n ≈ 0.5).
- Coincidence frequency f_c ≈ c²/(1.8·c_L·h): ≈12 kHz for 1 mm steel, ≈11 kHz for 3 mm MDF, ≈550 Hz for 40 mm wood. Thin skins radiate inefficiently below coincidence, so radiated sound is shaped by radiation efficiency as well as structural mode density. This is an SEA-standard relation; I did not find it in a source fetched this session.
- Why sparse, lightly damped modes "ring like sines": with fewer than one mode per ERB and long decays (steel t_e up to ~1.6 s), each mode is resolved by the ear as a pure tone. A real high-overlap panel puts tens of mutually beating modes into each band. The ear hears that as filtered noise whose band envelopes decay, not as pitches.

### Gaps
- I found no paper giving a perceptual threshold (modes per ERB, or M) at which a modal bank becomes indistinguishable from band-limited noise. The ERB comparison above is my inference.
- I found no measured loss factors or modal densities for actual building doors (steel hollow-metal, timber, glass) in the sources fetched.

## 3. Contact force, acceleration noise and contact duration

### Takeaway
Impact sound has two physical parts (Richards et al. 1979). **Ringing noise** comes from elastic vibration (modes). **Acceleration noise** is radiated by the sudden rigid-body acceleration of the bodies during the contact pulse. Contact duration τ sets the excitation bandwidth: a Hertz half-sine pulse of length τ is −3 dB at f ≈ 0.6/τ and has its first spectral null at f = 1.5/τ. Short, hard contacts (10–100 µs) excite everything and click. Soft or long contacts (≥1 ms) only reach the low modes and thud. Single ideal impulses sound "too clean". Real hard impacts are trains of micro-collisions over about 15 ms.

### Cited Findings
- Rigid-body sound has two sources, "ringing noise" [Richards et al. 1979b] from object vibration and "acceleration noise" [Richards et al. 1979a] produced when objects undergo rapid rigid-body acceleration. Rigid-body acceleration and modal vibration are linearly superposed in the radiated pressure, p = p_rigid + p_modal. — [Chadwick, Zheng & James 2012, ACM TOG 31(4)](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)
- Bibliography: E. J. Richards, M. E. Westcott & R. K. Jeyapalan, "On the prediction of impact noise, I: Acceleration noise", J. Sound Vib. 62(4):547–575 (1979), with a companion paper on ringing noise the same year. It introduced radiation-efficiency values for structural components in impact machines. — [search result record; see also citing work, Springer JMST 2011](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12206-011-0503-z)
- Hertz contact: f = K·d^1.5, with K = (4/3)·sqrt(r)·E* for spheres. The force is approximately a half-sine S(t) = sin(π(t−t₀)/τ) on [t₀, t₀+τ], with τ = 2.87·(m²/(r·E*²·V))^(1/5), where 1/r = 1/r₁+1/r₂, 1/m = 1/m₁+1/m₂, 1/E* = (1−ν₁²)/E₁ + (1−ν₂²)/E₂ and V = normal impact speed. To match the rigid-body solver's impulse j, scale the pulse by γ = π‖j‖/(2τ). "τ is typically in the range of 10–100 µs." Translational acceleration is aᵢ(t) = (γ/Mᵢ)·ĵ·S(t). For non-spherical bodies, spheres are fitted to the local surface curvature at the contact points to get τ. — [Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)
- Acceleration noise is radiated through precomputed directional pressure responses to short acceleration pulses ψ(t; h) in each of the 6 rigid DOFs. Base pulse time scale h = 24.322 µs. Varying h from 1× to 8× gave "little audible difference". A 5×10 angular grid was "nearly indistinguishable" from 40×80. — [Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)
- Results: acceleration noise "significantly complements the standard modal sound algorithm, especially for small objects". With it, coins and keys sound "significantly more crisp, detailed". For objects with interior cavities (a mug), the acceleration noise itself becomes pitched by interior reflections. Inter-object and environmental scattering matter for short transients: without them results can "sound somewhat harsh or abrupt", and two colliding steel spheres are "altered dramatically by the addition of environmental reverb". — [Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf); [project page](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/)
- Contact-force shape vs duration: "A simple function which has the qualitative correct form for an impact force is, for example, 1 − cos(2πt/τ) for 0 ≤ t ≤ τ… the exact details of the shape is relatively unimportant and the hardness is conveyed well by the duration." — [van den Doel et al. 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)
- Micro-collisions: "very hard" collisions such as a marble on stone have contact-break times around 50 µs, "however, such contacts sound too 'clean' in practice when represented by a single non-zero sample." Real hard impacts contain "sequences of very fast contact separations and collisions… caused by modal vibrations of the objects involved". These were simulated "by a short burst of impulse trains at the dominant modal frequencies… the microcollisions take place within 15 ms… An impact audio-force composed of impulses at the first 4 modal resonance frequencies sounded very convincing". — [van den Doel et al. 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)
- Nonlinear contact with dissipation (Hunt–Crossley, f = k·x^α + λ·x^α·ẋ, α = 1.5) produces a realistic, hysteretic attack transient. Its contact time and spectral tilt depend on hammer mass, stiffness k and velocity, not on a fixed pulse. — [Avanzini & Rocchesso 2001](https://avanzini.di.unimi.it/downloads/publications/avanzini_icmc01_finalsub.pdf)
- Zheng & James time-step contact/fracture at about 5 kHz "to resolve micro-collisions", with "hundreds of thousands of micro-impact problems" in large scenes. — [Zheng & James 2010](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/FractureSound/files/fractureSound_comp.pdf)

### Inferences (computed from the formulas above)
- Half-sine force of duration τ: |F(f)|/F(0) = |cos(πfτ)|/|1−(2fτ)²|. Computed roll-off: −3 dB at fτ ≈ 0.59, −6 dB at 0.82, −10 dB at 1.02, −20 dB at 1.30. First null at fτ = 1.5, then a −12 dB/oct envelope. For van den Doel's 1−cos (Hann) pulse: −3 dB at fτ ≈ 0.72, −6 dB at 1.0, −20 dB at 1.65, first null at fτ = 2.0. The Fourier transform of a half-sine pulse is tabulated in [Irvine, "The Fourier transform of a half-sine pulse"](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials_alt/hs_fourier.pdf). The roll-off points are my computation.
- Illustrative Hertz times (steel on steel, E* ≈ 115 GPa):
  - 50 g latch-bolt-like mass, 5 mm curvature, 0.5 m/s: τ ≈ 108 µs, −3 dB ≈ 5.5 kHz.
  - Same at 2 m/s: τ ≈ 82 µs.
  - 1 kg at 10 mm, 1 m/s: τ ≈ 270 µs, −3 dB ≈ 2.2 kHz.
  - 20 kg at 20 mm, 1 m/s: τ ≈ 780 µs, −3 dB ≈ 0.8 kHz.
  
  Small hard parts (latch bolt and strike, hinge pins, push-bar mechanism) give bright clicks that reach into the treble. A heavy leaf landing against a cushioned stop or seal (much longer τ, since seals and stops are soft) only excites low modes, which is the "thud". Effective mass, curvature and stop compliance for real doors are assumptions here, not sourced values.
- Hertz τ ∝ V^(−1/5): a 4× faster slam shortens τ by only 24 %. Most of the slam-speed dependence comes from amplitude (γ ∝ impulse) and from nonlinear/rattle effects, not from bandwidth.

## 4. SEA / energy-envelope transient synthesis, door-closing sound quality, band-envelope resynthesis

### Takeaway
For car doors the published synthesis approach is not modal. Bézat, Kronland-Martinet, Ystad et al. (PSA/LMA) split a door closure into several independent impacts with Empirical Mode Decomposition, then modelled each impact by a gain and a damping factor per ERB critical band. That is noise-band resynthesis that keeps band envelopes, and it was good enough to drive perceptual tests and real-time synthesis. Sound-quality work agrees on what reads as "solid": strong low-frequency content that is damped quickly, with little high-frequency content. Loudness (sone) and sharpness (acum) are the standard metrics. Rattles and harsh highs read as cheap.

### Cited Findings
- Bézat, Roussarie, Voinier, Kronland-Martinet & Ystad, "Car door closure sounds: characterization of perceptual properties through analysis-synthesis approach", ICA 2007 (Madrid). The sound was decomposed into several independent impact sources with Empirical Mode Decomposition (EMD). "Each impact was modeled by a set of gains and damping factors in each critical band (ERB), and these parameters were used to synthesize sounds with a real-time tool." — [HAL hal-00463358](https://hal.science/hal-00463358v1/document) (abstract via search; full text blocked by HAL bot protection)
- Bézat PhD thesis (Aix-Marseille 1, 2007, supervised by Kronland-Martinet and Ystad). Perceptual properties are split into analytical (sensory-panel), "natural" (source/event perception) and evocations. The analysis–synthesis model reproduces perceptual properties "from a reduced number of parameters", and was calibrated on the analytical properties. Generality was checked on another automotive impact (tapping noises). — [kronland.fr thesis page](http://kronland.fr/publications/perception-des-bruits-dimpact-application-au-bruit-de-fermeture-de-porte-automobile/); [HAL tel-00278427](https://hal.science/tel-00278427/)
- Door-closing sound quality: "door-closing sounds with abundant low-frequency contents and rapidly damped energy aroused feelings of solidity and the door being firmly closed", linked to security and luxury. "Abundant low-frequency contents and a smaller quantity of high-frequency contents… could help create 'massive' and 'not-harsh' door-closing sounds." — [Applied Acoustics, "Structural analysis of the value evaluation of vehicle door-closing sounds"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003682X18310132) (abstract-level via search)
- The most common door-closing metrics are loudness (sone) and sharpness (acum). A jury study used 7 cars with brands hidden, over headphones. — [Investigation of customer perception of vehicle door closing sound quality (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/317194387_INVESTIGATION_OF_CUSTOMER_PERCEPTION_OF_VEHICLE_DOOR_CLOSING_SOUND_QUALITY) (abstract-level)
- Ford (Scholl & Amman, SAE 1999-01-1682): door-closing transients "often possess complex internal structure resulting from impacts combined with mechanical and acoustic cavity resonances", spanning "ms to s" in time and about 100 Hz to about 10 kHz in frequency. They are visualised with translation-invariant wavelet scalograms. — [SAE 1999-01-1682](https://saemobilus.sae.org/papers/a-new-wavelet-technique-transient-sound-visualization-application-automotive-door-closing-events-1999-01-1682)
- Structure-borne door-closure sound quality was analysed with transient FEA+BEA only up to about 300 Hz (SAE 2005-01-2339). Deterministic CAE is limited to the low band, which is consistent with Section 2. — [SAE 2005-01-2339](https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/2005-01-2339)
- A sound-quality prediction model exists for the latch-closure transient specifically (SAE 2019-01-1517), so the latch is treated as a separable event. — [SAE 2019-01-1517](https://saemobilus.sae.org/content/2019-01-1517)
- SEA is energy-based and statistical (band-averaged energy, loss and coupling-loss factors). "Transient SEA methods have also been developed." — [Irvine SEA parameters](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials2/SEA_parameters_revX.pdf)
- Band-wise subtractive noise synthesis of impacts (time-varying filtering of noise to impose per-band damping) is the Aramaki & Kronland-Martinet (2006) approach that the Bézat door model builds on. — [Aramaki & Kronland-Martinet 2006](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/3457529_Analysis-Synthesis_of_impact_sound_by_real_time_dynamic_filtering)

### Inferences
- The car-door synthesis literature uses this structure: (a) a few discrete impact events (latch/striker, leaf onto seals or stops, secondary rattles), each separated in time; (b) for each event, per-ERB-band noise with a gain and an exponential decay rate. This maps directly onto the SEA picture. Above the modal-overlap frequency, a band's energy E_b(t) after an impulsive input decays as E_b(0)·exp(−2π·f_b·η_b·t) (from the SEA power balance with dissipation ω·η·E). Noise shaped per band with that envelope is a physical (SEA-derived) synthesis, not a fitted recipe: band gains come from input energy × coupling, band decays from η(f). The exponential band-decay formula is my derivation from the SEA energy balance in Irvine; no fetched paper states it for door synthesis.
- A hybrid that follows from Sections 2–4: explicit modes only where M < 1 (low-order leaf/panel modes, for a steel door the lowest tens of Hz, for a solid timber leaf up to the low kHz), plus per-band decaying noise above, plus an acceleration-noise/contact click per contact event, plus micro-collision trains for hard metal-on-metal contacts.
- "Solid" corresponds to short low-frequency decay plus low sharpness. "Cheap/tinny" corresponds to long high-frequency ringing and rattles. A modal-only steel door with lightly damped high modes is the "tinny" end of this scale.

## 5. Practical implementation: noise-excited resonator banks, dense banks, rattles, non-repetition

### Takeaway
The practical toolkit in the literature:
- an excitation signal: physical contact force, micro-collision bursts, or noise; never a bare unit impulse
- through modal resonators (for the sparse low modes) and band-limited noise with per-band decays (for the dense region)
- randomised mode gains and phases, random dip filters, stochastic grain triggering (Poisson events with exponentially decaying energy) for rattles and variation
- level-of-detail by dropping the lowest-power modes

### Cited Findings
- Resonator implementation: complex oscillator per mode, c_m(k) = c_m(k−1)·Δc_m, with Δc_m = e^(2πi·f_m/f_s), output Im{c_m}, SIMD 4-wide blocks. Mix low-frequency modes at lower sample rates (multirate gives up to 2× speedup). Use a CPU-capped mode quota: drop modes by power, the typical effect being "a reduction in high frequencies". 10 % of one Xbox 360 CPU handled "dozens of simultaneous impact sounds". — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)
- Variation by random mode gains (the effect of striking at random locations): g_m(v) = lerp(1, ξ, α(v))/c(v), with ξ ~ U[0,1], α(v) = bias(v, 0.9), bias(x,b) = x/((1/b − 2)(1 − x) + 1), and power normalisation c(v) = sqrt(1 − α + α²/3). v ∈ [0,1] sets the variation amount. — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)
- Randomise initial phases φ₀,m: listeners are mostly phase-insensitive, and identical phases make simultaneous impacts line up and clip. Add small random onset delays to spread coincident impacts. — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)
- Variation for non-modal/broadband sounds: a cascade of biquad dip (cut) filters at random log-spaced centre frequencies, with random gain and random Q in designer-set ranges. "We have obtained good results with about 10 filters." This works best for broadband sounds "difficult to represent with a collection of narrow band modes". — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf). Ren et al. note that this random-dip residual variation "does not transfer accordingly with the geometry change or the dynamics" — [Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)
- Position-dependent timbre: hitting near an edge sounds higher-pitched, since low frequencies are attenuated in fine structures or near edges. A heuristic is a high-pass with cutoff stored per surface location. — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf). Physically this is the location-dependent gain matrix A (a_{nk}) in [van den Doel et al. 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)
- Micro-collision excitation: a burst of impulse trains at the first 4 modal frequencies within about 15 ms, instead of a single sample. — [van den Doel et al. 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)
- Scraping/sliding excitation: "phonograph needle" model over a surface profile generated at spatial resolution v_max/f_s. The audio-force combines a roughness model (fractal/noise profile) and an interaction model. — [van den Doel et al. 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf). Picard's thesis used a Perlin-noise audio-force (octaves of interpolated noise with frequency 2^i and decreasing amplitude) as a cheap roughness excitation — [Picard thesis 2009, ch. 4](http://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2009/Pic09/Thesis_CPicard_AftDS.pdf)
- Granular rattle/shaker model (PhISEM): Poisson-distributed collision events whose rate and amplitude follow exponentially decaying system energy. Each event is a short decaying noise burst through a few resonant filters. — [Csound STKShakers](https://csound.com/docs/manual/STKShakers.html); [Cook 1997 CMJ 21(3)](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Physically-Informed-Sonic-Modeling-(PhISM):-of-Cook/91a5ea5d5aa6acf3e004c75302896d70c0ff43f5)
- Grain-based non-repetition: grains of 10–100 ms from spectral-flux segmentation, Tukey taper r = 0.02, chosen randomly or by correlation pattern, triggered on penetration-force peaks. — [Picard thesis 2009](http://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2009/Pic09/Thesis_CPicard_AftDS.pdf). Dictionary atoms plus a random perturbation vector inside a radius-R sphere — [Ahmad & Kondoz DAFx-11](http://recherche.ircam.fr/pub/dafx11/Papers/92_e.pdf)
- Attack handling in block/FFT synthesis: start the first frame with a rectangular sub-window, not a Hann ramp, or the attack loses crispness. — [Bonneel et al. 2008](https://www-sop.inria.fr/reves/Basilic/2008/BDTVJ08/FastModalSounds.pdf)
- Contact-force timing must run at audio rate. Physics-rate (60 Hz) impulses must be turned into continuous force pulses (Hertz half-sine scaled by γ = π‖j‖/2τ) before driving the resonators. — [Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf); [van den Doel et al. 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)
- Envelope realism: sampled envelopes beat pure exponentials. Any non-exponential behaviour (beating between close modes, two-stage decay) needs either sampled envelopes or many closely spaced modes that beat naturally. — [Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)
- Short transients need environment and scattering, or they sound harsh. — [Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)

### Inferences
- A dense modal region can be approximated cheaply by exciting a bank of band-pass filters (one per ERB or third-octave) with the contact force plus a little noise. Each band's decay would be set from η(f) (for example the panel law η = 1.8/f^0.87, or measured values), with T60 per band = 2.2/(f·η) (Irvine's room relation η = 2.2/(f·T_R) inverted). This gives the "dense, non-tonal" behaviour a 1 mm steel skin should have (M ≈ 2–3) without thousands of oscillators. The equivalence to a dense modal bank holds when each band holds many modes (n·ERB ≫ 1, true for thin skins). This is my synthesis of the cited pieces, not a tested method from one paper.
- For non-repetition that stays physical: vary the contact location (gain matrix), impact velocity (τ ∝ V^(−1/5), amplitude ∝ impulse), micro-collision count and timing, and random noise seeds per event. Use random dip filters only as a last resort, since Ren et al. note they do not follow the physics.
- For rattles from loose parts (push-bar linkage, hardware), PhISEM-style Poisson events driven by the leaf's decaying vibration energy is the published structure. Each event should be a small hard contact (short τ click plus a few high modes).

### Gaps
- I found no published listening test comparing "noise through dense filter bank" against "thousands of modes" for the same plate. The equivalence above is inferred.
- I found no published numbers for rattle event rates or energy thresholds in door hardware.
