# Fill the band, separate the events, ring the box

Our door renders sound sterile and tonal mainly because they carry far too few modes. A real door hit is spectrally noise-like within 50 ms of onset, and a few dozen damped sinusoids cannot get there. The published fix is to add a non-modal layer to the modal bank. For an engine that must model the object rather than fit a recording, the physical form of that layer is: modes populated at the density the geometry actually has; SEA-style band decays where modes overlap; an audio-rate contact force with acceleration noise and micro-collisions for small hard parts; and a mechanism with real clearances. Our own measurement of the reference recordings gives the size of the gap. Spectral flatness in the 50 ms after onset (geometric over arithmetic mean of a 1024-point power spectrum, about 0.2–17 kHz) is **0.1–0.3 for real push-bar actuation and fire-door closing hits and about 0.11 for a residential door's main closing hit, against 0.000–0.003 for our modal renders**. On structure, real residential closes show **1–4 precursor latch events 40–150 ms before the main hit (40–90 ms even on a fast slam)** and a **settle train of sub-events every 30–40 ms, each a few dB down, lasting 0.4–0.8 s**. Real push-bar events ring **100–300 ms** (room included). Published levels are sparse and weak, but they put a normal latching close at roughly 70–82 dB(A) at 1 m, a slam 11–14 dB higher, and a conventional push bar near 73–79 dB. The plan at the end turns each of these into a change to the simulation with a number to hit and a test that shows whether it worked.

## Modal synthesis stops working above a modal overlap of one

Every published system that comes close to recordings adds something non-modal to the bank of damped sinusoids. Ren, Yeh and Lin put it bluntly: "linear modal synthesis alone is not capable of synthesizing sounds that are as rich and realistic as many real-world recordings". They explain that "during the short period of contact, not all energy is transformed into stable vibration that can be represented with a small number of damped sinusoids" ([Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)). The fixes fall into two groups. The data-driven ones are a recorded residual (Serra–Smith SMS; [Lloyd, Raghuvanshi & Govindaraju 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf); Ren 2013) and transient grains cut from recordings ([Picard, Tsingos & Faure 2009](https://hal.science/inria-00394469)). The physical ones are Chadwick's rigid-body acceleration noise ([Chadwick, Zheng & James 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)), nonlinear mode coupling in thin shells ([Chadwick, An & James 2009](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/HarmonicShells/HarmonicShells09.pdf)), per-band dynamically filtered noise ([Aramaki & Kronland-Martinet 2006](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/3457529_Analysis-Synthesis_of_impact_sound_by_real_time_dynamic_filtering)), micro-collision trains ([van den Doel, Kry & Pai 2001](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)) and Cook's stochastic particle events (PhISEM, [Csound STKShakers](https://csound.com/docs/manual/STKShakers.html)). Lloyd's game system shipped in Crackdown 2. With sampled per-mode envelopes plus a residual it produced sounds "in most cases … indistinguishable from recorded clips", and the residual turned out to be "mostly noise concentrated near the attack. The tail is empty" ([Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)). That puts the non-modal energy in the first 5–30 ms, which is exactly the window where our renders measure as pure tones. Our project rule is to model the thing and not the sound, which rules out the recording-based fixes and leaves the physical ones. Those are the ones this report builds on.

The reason modal-only synthesis fails is modal overlap. The modal overlap factor M = n·η·f (n modes per Hz, η loss factor) is the ratio of each mode's damping bandwidth to the spacing between modes. Above M ≈ 1 the response becomes a smooth statistical continuum. That regime belongs to SEA, not to a deterministic list of modes ([Irvine, SEA parameters](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials2/SEA_parameters_revX.pdf)). For a plate, the generic bending modal density reduces to **n(f) = √3·S/(h·c_L)**, which matches Irvine's worked 0.068 modes/Hz for 5 mm aluminium at 1 m² ([Irvine, Modal Density](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials_alt/mdens.pdf)). Applied to door leaves:

| Body (assumed dims) | n (modes/Hz) | Mode spacing | Modes below 17 kHz | Where M crosses 1 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steel door skin, 1 mm, 1.8 m², c_L ≈ 5400 m/s | **0.58** | 1.7 Hz | ≈ 9,800 | below ~100 Hz (M ≈ 1.9 at 100 Hz, 2.6 at 1 kHz with η = 1.8/f^0.87) |
| Hollow-core leaf, 3 mm skins (c_L ≈ 2000 assumed) | ≈ 0.5 | 2 Hz | ≈ 8,800 | low hundreds of Hz |
| Solid timber leaf, 40 mm (c_L ≈ 3000 assumed) | **0.026** | 38 Hz | ≈ 440 | ~3.8 kHz at η = 0.01, ~770 Hz at η = 0.05 |
| Push-bar rail shell, ~2 mm Al, ~0.2 m² (assumed) | ≈ 0.03 | ~30 Hz | ≈ 550 | depends on η; kHz range |

The densities and overlaps in the table are worked from Irvine's formulas and loss-factor law; they are inferences, not door measurements. No measured modal data for building doors turned up. The conclusion still holds: a steel skin or hollow-core skin is a statistical radiator across nearly the whole audible band, with about 75 modes inside each auditory filter at 1 kHz (ERB ≈ 133 Hz, [Glasberg & Moore 1990](https://doi.org/10.1016/0378-5955(90)90170-T)). A solid timber leaf really is modal up to the low kHz. A bank of a few dozen lightly damped modes puts fewer than one mode in each ERB, so the ear resolves each one as a pure tone. That is the "too tonal and synthetic" verdict. Rayleigh damping makes it worse, because it forces every mode onto one damping curve, "impossible to make … exactly the same as an arbitrary real-world recording" ([Ren et al. 2013](http://gamma-web.iacs.umd.edu/AUDIO_MATERIAL/examplebasedsoundsynthesis.pdf)). Ren's fitted metal values (α = 6.30, β = 2.12e-8) give T60 ≈ 2 s at 1 kHz. That is a bell, not a cored door.

To link our flatness measurement to mode count, we computed (our own computation) the flatness of synthetic modal sums in the same 1024-point, 0.2–17 kHz window at 48 kHz. Every count up to 200 log-spaced modes gave **≤ 0.005**, which is the range of our renders. A plate-like uniform density of 0.026 modes/Hz (about one mode per FFT bin, ~520 modes to 20 kHz) gave **0.32 with a flat spectrum and 0.19 with a −3 dB/octave tilt**, which is the range of the real recordings. A density of 0.1 modes/Hz at −3 dB/oct gave 0.35, and 0.58 modes/Hz with a flat spectrum gave 0.55. Steeper tilt pulls flatness down whatever the density: red noise at −6 dB/oct measures only 0.06. So the real values of 0.1–0.3 mean broadband energy that fills the band evenly with a moderate tilt. That energy has to come from the mode density a plate really has, or from a broadband contact transient. Spreading more modes logarithmically will not reach it. The table also explains why our earlier octave-band-noise house door was rejected as "grainy, wood and steel the same": noise is the right model for a steel skin, which has M > 1, and the wrong one for a solid timber leaf, which has M < 1 through the band where its knock lives.

The contact itself matters as well. Chadwick et al. split impact sound into **ringing noise** (modes) and **acceleration noise**, the pressure radiated by the sudden rigid-body acceleration of the parts. For small hard objects such as coins and dice, acceleration noise is most of the click: "modal synthesis alone sound[s] muffled and provide[s] little temporal distinction" ([Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)). A latch bolt, a strike lip and a push-bar crank are objects of that kind. Hertz contact sets the bandwidth. A half-sine force pulse of length τ is −3 dB at f ≈ 0.59/τ and has its first null at 1.5/τ (our computation from the transform tabulated by [Irvine](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials_alt/hs_fourier.pdf)), and Chadwick's τ formula gives values "typically in the range of 10–100 µs". Worked for steel on steel, a 50 g bolt-like mass at 0.5 m/s gives τ ≈ 108 µs, −3 dB near 5.5 kHz; a 20 kg mass on 20 mm curvature gives τ ≈ 780 µs, −3 dB near 0.8 kHz. Because τ ∝ V^(−1/5), a 4× faster slam shortens contact by only 24 %. Slam loudness therefore comes from amplitude and rattle, not from added bandwidth. Hard contacts also bounce. A single-sample hard impact "sound[s] too 'clean'", and van den Doel et al. found that "an impact audio-force composed of impulses at the first 4 modal resonance frequencies", with the micro-collisions inside 15 ms, "sounded very convincing" ([FoleyAutomatic](https://www8.cs.umu.se/kurser/TDBD12/HT01/papers/foleyautomatic.pdf)).

The car-door literature, the only body of door-synthesis research found, is not modal at all. Bézat, Kronland-Martinet, Ystad et al. used Empirical Mode Decomposition to split a closure into several independent impacts. They modelled each impact "by a set of gains and damping factors in each critical band (ERB)" and drove a real-time synthesiser from those parameters ([Bézat et al., ICA 2007](https://hal.science/hal-00463358v1/document)). Read through SEA, that is physics: above the overlap frequency, band energy after an impulse decays as exp(−2π·f·η·t), so T60 = 2.2/(f·η). This step is our derivation from Irvine's energy balance. The same studies found that what reads as "solid" is "abundant low-frequency contents and rapidly damped energy" ([Applied Acoustics](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0003682X18310132)). Long high-frequency ringing and rattles read as cheap.

## A door close is a timed chain of contacts, and the gaps are millimetres over edge speed

The published geometry is small. A residential tubular latch throws 1/2 in (12.7 mm) ([Accurate Lock](https://accuratelockandhardware.com/product/tubularlatch/)); a Von Duprin 99 exit latch throws 3/4 in (19 mm) ([TruDoor](https://www.trudoor.com/products/von-duprin-99eo-panic-rim-exit-device)). The door face sits 1/16–3/32 in (1.6–2.4 mm) off the stop ([ANSI/SDI A250.8](https://steeldoor.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/02/A250_8-1.pdf)). Strike reveal is at most 1/8 in (3.2 mm), and BHMA's deadlatching test adds exactly that much slop ([BHMA A156.3 draft](https://buildershardware.com/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=kJ3qTCDM_Ew%3D&portalid=0)). Rubber silencers give a 1/16–1/8 in cushion and "provide constant tension for door latches" ([RBA Door](https://www.rbadoor.com/door-silencer-for-metal-frames.html)). Because every gap is millimetres, the time between events is the gap divided by the edge speed. The event chain for one closing swing, derived from that geometry, runs: bevel tip meets strike lip (an impact, then a scrape as the bolt is cammed back over about the throw); deadlatch plunger pressed in (a second tick, on deadlatches only); bolt passes the lip and snaps out, hitting its stop in the latch case after a guessed 5–15 ms of spring travel; leaf meets stop or silencers about 2 mm later; leaf rebounds by up to the 3.2 mm strike clearance, and the flat face of the bolt clacks against the keeper; then a decaying settle between stop and keeper, plus looser rattles from hinge knuckles, roses and handles.

| Edge speed at strike | Bevel ride (12.7 mm) | Snap → stop (2 mm) | What it sounds like |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0.06 m/s (closer latch zone) | ≈ 210 ms | ≈ 33 ms | scrape, distinct click, soft landing |
| 0.15–0.3 m/s (normal to firm hand) | ≈ 40–85 ms | ≈ 7–13 ms | click train, then thump |
| 2 m/s (car-door-class slam) | ≈ 6 ms | ≈ 1 ms | fused |

The table is derived; nobody publishes measured building-door edge speeds. The only slam figures are automotive: 30–90°/s over the last 5° counts as slamming ([US10392849B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10392849B2/en)), and car doors latch at 0.8–1.2 m/s ([door closing velocity study](https://airccse.org/journal/ijci/papers/4215ijci01.pdf)). **Our recordings contradict the "fused on a slam" row.** Real residential closes put 1–4 precursor latch events 40–150 ms before the main hit, and 40–90 ms even on fast slams. If the first precursor is the bevel striking the lip, about one throw (12.7–15 mm) before seating, then the edge speed at the strike is about **0.15–0.35 m/s even on a hard close**, an order of magnitude below car-door speeds. The alternative reading is that some precursors are not latch contacts. Either way, the engine should never let latch and leaf events fuse for hand-closed residential doors. If ours does, then either its edge speed at the strike is too high, or its bevel contact makes no audible impact (a smooth sliding constraint instead of a hit followed by a scrape), or both.

Our settle train (sub-events every 30–40 ms, a few dB down each, lasting 0.4–0.8 s) carries a second physical clue. A rigid leaf bouncing ballistically in a clearance would give intervals that shrink geometrically, as in the Rath–Rocchesso bouncer, where t_int(n) = (√C)ⁿ·t_int(0) ([The Sounding Object, ch. 9](https://www.soundobject.org/SObBook/SObBook_JUL03.pdf)). A near-constant spacing means a spring sets the period: the leaf, or the bolt in its keeper, oscillates against a compliant stop or flexes in a low mode, and clips against the keeper once per cycle. That is an impact oscillator. Taking 30–40 ms as one cycle (25–33 Hz) and an effective latch-edge mass of m/3 for a rigid rotor (about 4 kg hollow-core, 10 kg solid), the restoring stiffness comes to about **100–430 N/mm** (our computation). That is plausible for silencers or weatherstrip in series with leaf flex, but unverified. A drop of 2–4 dB per event is an amplitude ratio of 0.63–0.79 per cycle, an equivalent Q of about 7–14. Seventeen-odd events at about 3 dB each cover 0.6 s and 50 dB, which matches the measured duration. The car-door beamforming study shows the same kind of structure: secondary events about 15 ms apart, and a sub-200 Hz pattern that "repeats every 20 ms, eight times" with falling strength ([beamforming study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/297155286_Visualization_of_door-slam_sound_by_using_beamforming)). A well-adjusted strike, with the latch snug and the leaf on its silencers, almost removes this train ([iDigHardware](https://idighardware.com/2013/10/ff-strike-latch-guard/)). Strike clearance is therefore the physical control for jiggle. A sim with a rigid leaf, stiff and heavily damped Hunt–Crossley stops and no clearance will settle in one hit, which is the "no post-shut jiggle" verdict.

Opening a knob door is a shorter chain. A conventional lock turns about 52° to retract fully, and some latches about 60° ([US11585115](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11585115)). Levers carry a stronger return spring, with release torque up to 50 lbf·in against 18 for knobs ([BHMA A156.3](https://buildershardware.com/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=kJ3qTCDM_Ew%3D&portalid=0)). The derived sequence is: spindle lost-motion ticks; spring wind-up; the bolt dragging across the keeper under silencer or closer preload; a small knock as the leaf jumps the preload distance; and on release, the bolt shooting to full throw plus the lever hitting its rose stop within a few ms. Von Duprin fits a "damper-controlled lever return" precisely because that return is a noise source ([Von Duprin QM](https://www.vonduprin.com/en/products/options-accessories/quiet-monitoring.html)).

The push bar is a chain of metal end-stops radiated by a hollow box and by the door skin it is bolted to. The Von Duprin 99 pad travels 3/4 in (projection 3-13/16 in at rest, 3-1/16 in depressed), and its centre case is 8 × 2-3/4 × 2-3/8 in ([TruDoor](https://www.trudoor.com/products/von-duprin-99eo-panic-rim-exit-device)). Releasing the latch may take no more than 15 lbf (67 N), or 50 lbf with 250 lbf of crowd load on the door ([BHMA A156.3](https://buildershardware.com/LinkClick.aspx?fileticket=kJ3qTCDM_Ew%3D&portalid=0)). Inside, bell cranks drive a drive bar against a return spring, which pulls the latch retractor; bumpers on quiet units engage about 0.1 in before bottoming ([US11220838B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11220838B2/en)). The noise sources the patents name are latchbolt impact on the strike, contact between components, "rattle from clearance fits", metal-on-metal impacts, and links returning to rest under spring force. The free spring return of the latchbolt makes parts "impact or grind against one another" ([US11156025B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US11156025B2/en)). Von Duprin's fluid damper exists so "the pad settles back without the metallic snap you get from an undamped device" ([Von Duprin](https://www.vonduprin.com/en/products/options-accessories/quiet-monitoring.html)). The derived sequence is: palm slap on the pad; a 19 mm stroke in about 50–150 ms with crank and drive-bar travel and the latch dragging out of the keeper; the pad bottoming (the "chunk"); the leaf released by a millimetre or two of preload; then, on hand release, pad, cranks and drive bar snapping back to rest and the latch shooting to full throw against nothing. The case is 203 × 70 × 60 mm, so its air cavity has first axial modes near **845, 2450 and 2860 Hz** (c/2L, our computation). A rail interior about 0.8 m long (assumed) adds a series starting near 214 Hz. Its thin walls are a dense shell. The whole device sits on a 0.58 modes/Hz steel skin, which acts as a soundboard. Chadwick found that acceleration noise from a body with an interior cavity "becomes pitched by interior reflections" ([Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)). An engine that models the push bar as a beam gets sparse bending modes in near-n² ratios, the glockenspiel signature of a "thin bar". The real device is a box resonator plus a dense shell plus a door panel.

A hydraulic closer sets the steel door's closing speeds. ADA requires at least 5 s from 90° to 12° ([ADA 404.2.8](https://www.doorclosersusa.com/ADA-Door-Closing-Speed-ADA-404-2-8-and-404-2-9-Door-Clo-s/36251.htm)). LCN calls 5–7 s "normal … evenly divided between main speed and latch speed" ([LCN 4040XP](https://us.allegion.com/content/dam/allegion-us-2/web-documents-2/InstallInstructions/LCN_4040XP_Series_Pull_Side_Mount_Installation_Instructions_107160.pdf)). The latch valve governs the last 10–15° ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Door_closer)). For a 36 in leaf that works out to a sweep of about 0.3–0.45 m/s at the edge and a latch zone of about **0.05–0.08 m/s**, so a 19 mm exit latch rides its strike for 0.25–0.4 s and snaps 20–50 ms before the leaf reaches the silencers (derived). Steel doors weigh about 3.5 lb/ft², roughly 32–33 kg for 36 × 80 in ([doorclosersusa](https://www.doorclosersusa.com/How-Much-Does-a-Hollow-Metal-Door-Weigh--s/36028.htm)). Many steel frames are left ungrouted and hollow ([SDI FAQ](https://steeldoor.org/faqs/)), which makes the frame a second ringing body. Silencers, three on the strike jamb, prevent "metal-on-metal contact" between those two bodies.

## A latching close is 70–82 dB(A) at 1 m, and the latch carries much of it

No peer-reviewed measurement of a building door at a stated distance and weighting turned up, so every figure below is a bracket with caveats. The best paired data come from a door-silencer patent: one interior bathroom door, metered at 7 ft, mean of three trials, with trials agreeing within 3 dB ([US 11,674,342 B2](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11674342)). The weighting is unstated, and the 63 dB "bedroom ambient" suggests C/Z weighting or a phone meter. The absolute values are therefore inflated, but the differences between conditions are usable.

| Event | Reported value | 1 m estimate (our conversion) | Source and caveat |
|---|---|---|---|
| Interior door, normal latching close | 84 dB at 2.1 m | ≤ 90.6 (upper bound) | [US 11,674,342](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/11674342); weighting unknown |
| Same, slam | 95 dB at 2.1 m | ≤ 101.6 | same |
| Same with latch-bolt control | 72 light / 88 slam | ≤ 78.6 / ≤ 94.6 | same |
| Conventional push bar, hospital | +29 to +35 dB over 44.2 dB ambient (≈ 73–79) | ≈ 73–79 if metered near 1 m | [US 10,907,377](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10907377); distance and weighting not stated |
| Damped "quiet" panic device | +0.01 to +15 dB over ambient | – | same; patent claim |
| Conventional latch and handle, opening | "over 30 additional decibels" over 40–42 | ≈ 70–75 | same |
| Car driver door, 10 cars, 123 closings | 72.4 dB LAFmax at 3 m; LWA 91 | **≈ 82–83 LAFmax** | [Styria 2023](https://www.umwelt.steiermark.at/cms/dokumente/12873382_25545/03653fa2/ABT15-SEL_SchallEmi_PKWT%C3%BCren_V1-2023.pdf); best-measured door event |
| Door closing, controlled / fast / wind slam | 55–65 / 70–80 / 90–100+ | – | [Waterson](https://watersonusa.com/solutions/wind-tunnel-effect); marketing, no method |

Putting these together, defensible working targets at 1 m, A-weighted Fast, are **60–70 dB for a gentle close, 70–82 dB for a normal latching close, and 85–98 dB for a hard slam**, with a push-bar actuation and return at about 73–79 dB. The slam bracket fits the car-door anchor (a 20–30 kg door into rubber at ~1 m/s gives ~82 dB) and the patent deltas. The slam–normal difference is +11 dB undamped, which matches 20·log10 of a 3–4× speed ratio (+10–12 dB). Hertzian contact shortening adds a little A-weighted brightness on top, so +11 to +14 dB is a fair bracket for the speed law. Single car closings varied by up to about 21 dB across cars and about 15 dB within one car ([Styria 2023](https://www.umwelt.steiermark.at/cms/dokumente/12873382_25545/03653fa2/ABT15-SEL_SchallEmi_PKWT%C3%BCren_V1-2023.pdf)), so a game should spread closings by at least ±5 dB through the physics, not through a dice roll on gain.

The patent's attribution is more useful than its absolute levels. **Controlling the latch bolt removed 12 dB from a light close and 7 dB from a slam; jamb pads removed only 2–3 dB.** Taken at face value, the latch and strike are the largest single source in a residential close: on the light close the leaf alone reaches 72 against 84 for the whole event. A sim whose latch click is a quiet tick under the leaf thump has the balance backwards. The data are a single low-quality patent, but they point the same way as the Foley convention of a "crack" from latch on strike before the "boom" ([sfxengine](https://sfxengine.com/blog/door-slam-sound-effect), low-authority).

Two calibration traps apply. First, for a click much shorter than the 125 ms Fast time constant, LAFmax ≈ SEL + 9 dB and **Lpeak ≈ LAFmax + 8 to 14 dB** for effective durations of 5–20 ms. The game should calibrate by an integrated or Fast-weighted level, not by sample peak. Second, recordings carry their room. QMUL's QuAP study raised realism ratings in 5 of 6 procedural classes with post-production alone (reverb, compression, EQ), and its users preferred layering synthesis with recorded material ([arXiv 2606.00629](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.00629)). Bézat found the room "decreases drastically the discrimination of the sounds" ([Bézat et al. 2006](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1003.4908)). Chadwick reported that short transients without environmental scattering "sound somewhat harsh or abrupt" ([Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)). A dry render judged against room recordings will read as "too short" partly for that reason. The 100–300 ms push-bar ring in our measurement includes the room.

## The procedural literature supplies the structures; the physics supplies the numbers

Andy Farnell's *Designing Sound* has no door-slam practical, but his creak, switch and clock chapters give a consistent architecture: several excitation sources feeding one shared body. The creaking door is stick-slip impulses into six wooden formants (62.5–790 Hz, Q 1–3) and then eight parallel delay resonators of 4.52–16 ms, a rectangular-membrane series on a 125 Hz fundamental. He suggests "adding another excitation source such as a door handle or lock to the same resonator to get an integrated effect" ([Farnell, pp. 395–400](https://wp.ufpel.edu.br/labcomp/files/2022/09/PureData-Designing_Sound-Andy_Farnell.pdf)). His switch click is noise band-passed at 5 kHz, Q 12, with a 1 ms attack and 20 ms decay. On its own it "lacks complexity", and "a big difference in the total effect occurs once you have three or four clicks in close time proximity". Very short band-passed noise bursts also vary randomly in level because a short segment may miss the band's energy. His fix is to put the filters before the envelope, or to use deterministic excitation ([Farnell pp. 487–494](https://wp.ufpel.edu.br/labcomp/files/2022/09/PureData-Designing_Sound-Andy_Farnell.pdf)). That level lottery is a plausible cause of the "grainy" push-bar complaint in our earlier round. His clock chapter adds that the micro-events inside one mechanical tick must keep fixed, deterministic timing, and that the mechanism parts "sound dry and lifeless" until a body resonator "bring[s] the sound to life". All three points carry over to a physical engine: separate contacts sharing a body, three or four close sub-events per click, and no white-noise bursts shorter than the band can fill.

FoleyAutomatic contributes the audio-rate audio-force: hardness is conveyed by contact duration more than by pulse shape, and the 15 ms micro-collision burst covers hard contacts ([van den Doel et al. 2001](http://www.cs.ubc.ca/~kvdoel/publications/foleyautomatic.pdf)). The Sounding Object contributes the elasto-plastic friction model behind its squeaky swinging door, f = σ₀z + σ₁ż + σ₂v + σ₃w, which yields glissandi that a fixed stick-slip clock cannot. It also gives the parameter guide: normal force gives "rougher and louder sounds", and the noise coefficient sets perceived roughness ([Rocchesso & Fontana 2003, ch. 8](https://www.soundobject.org/SObBook/SObBook_JUL03.pdf)). Our LuGre hinge belongs to this family already. Cook's PhISEM is a Poisson collision process riding on exponentially decaying system energy. Each collision adds to a fast-decaying excitation, which is filtered through a few resonances. The STK constants are per sample, for example tambourine 32 objects, sound decay 0.95, system decay 0.9985, resonances 2300/5600/8100 Hz ([STK Shakers.cpp](https://github.com/thestk/stk/blob/master/src/Shakers.cpp)). PhISEM suits many-particle textures. A loose handle or a bolt in an oversized keeper is one or two bodies in a constrained gap, which is closer to Rath and Rocchesso's irregular bouncer: geometric decay of intervals and velocities, random shortening below the bound, modal weights that change per hit, and a stop threshold where the parts "finally stay in constant contact, a clear difference to simply retriggering" ([The Sounding Object, pp. 182–187](https://www.soundobject.org/SObBook/SObBook_JUL03.pdf)). Their perceptual warning is that temporal pattern alone separates bouncing from breaking. A rattle that is too regular reads as a ball, and one that is dense at onset with quickly falling density reads as breakage.

Lloyd's engineering numbers transfer even though his residual does not. A complex-oscillator mode costs one complex multiply per sample, SIMD runs 4 modes wide, low modes can run at a lower sample rate, and a quota drops the lowest-power modes first. Dozens of simultaneous impacts fit in 10 % of one Xbox 360 core ([Lloyd et al. 2011](http://www.nikunjr.com/Papers/5.pdf)). Random initial phases keep simultaneous voices from aligning and clipping. Moffat and Reiss found additive synthesis to be the only technique not significantly different from the recorded reference across all their classes ([ACM TAP 2018](https://dl.acm.org/doi/abs/10.1145/3165287)). That supports spending the CPU on many modes rather than on noise tricks where a body really is modal.

## An engine plan in eight changes with numbers

The plan keeps every element physical: rigid leaf, plate modes with radiation, Hunt–Crossley contacts, LuGre hinge, sprung latch, silencers, closer. It changes resolution, density and mechanism so the missing properties come out of the object's state.

**1. Resolve contacts at audio rate.** Evaluate Hunt–Crossley force for metal–metal contacts at the output rate with 2–4× substeps, because a 50 g bolt on a steel strike has τ ≈ 80–110 µs (four to five samples at 48 kHz), and a physics-rate impulse cannot excite above about 0.59/τ. Target contact durations are about 100 µs for bolt on strike or keeper, about 0.3 ms for push-bar crank stops (assumed by mass), and 1 ms or more for a leaf on rubber silencers. The force pulse, not a unit impulse, drives every body.

**2. Populate modes at the density the geometry has, and drop Rayleigh damping.** For each radiating body, compute n(f) = √3·S/(h·c_L) and place modes at random spacing, uniform in frequency, up to about 17 kHz. Cap the explicit set at roughly one effective mode per 40 Hz (about 400–550 per body), and give each effective mode the summed energy of the real modes it stands for. Our flatness test shows this density lands at 0.19–0.32 in the measurement window. A solid timber leaf needs no aggregation, since its physical density is already about 0.026 modes/Hz, roughly 440 modes. Below about 1 kHz, keep at least 3–5 modes per ERB on high-overlap bodies so low bands do not resolve into tones. This threshold is our inference; no published perceptual figure exists. Cost runs to about 20–25 M complex updates per second per body, which is affordable for a few door voices with SIMD. Set damping per body from η(f) and give up the single Rayleigh curve. For a cored hollow-metal leaf, the built-up sandwich law (η = 0.05 below 500 Hz, 0.05·500/f above) gives T60 = 2.2/(f·η) ≈ 0.44 s at 100 Hz, 0.22 s at 200 Hz and **≈ 90 ms flat above 500 Hz**. For the bare push-bar rail and the hollow frame, the thin-panel law η = 1.8/f^0.87 (floor 0.002) gives 0.5–0.6 s up to 1 kHz and 0.28 s at 4 kHz ([Irvine](https://www.vibrationdata.com/tutorials2/SEA_parameters_revX.pdf)). A composite of those lands in the measured 100–300 ms. For wood, Ren's fitted α = 2.14, β = 3.08e-6 gives T60 ≈ 0.42 s at 500 Hz, 0.11 s at 1 kHz and 28 ms at 2 kHz, which is why a timber knock is short and pitched. If CPU forces it on the steel skin, replace the aggregated modes above the overlap frequency with per-ERB noise. That noise should be filtered before the envelope (Farnell), carry the SEA band decay, and be scaled by the band energy of the actual contact force. In a band with M > 1, the impulse response really is statistically Gaussian noise, so this is not a fitted recipe.

**3. Add acceleration noise for small hard parts.** The latch bolt, deadlatch plunger, push-bar pad, cranks and drive bar are compact bodies whose own modes lie mostly above or at the top of hearing. Their click is the pressure radiated by rigid-body acceleration during the contact pulse ([Chadwick et al. 2012](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/projects/Sound/impact/PrecomputedAccelerationNoise2012.pdf)). A real-time approximation (our inference, not from the paper) is a compact dipole whose output follows the time derivative of each part's acceleration during the Hertz pulse, scaled by part volume, aimed along the contact normal, and added linearly to the modal pressure. That puts a broadband, non-tonal edge on every latch event, which is what the 50 ms flatness window measures.

**4. Let micro-collisions happen.** Couple the strike and keeper plate's first few modes back into the bolt contact gap at audio rate. The bolt then re-contacts on the plate's vibration, as van den Doel describes. If the coupled solve is too costly, emit a burst of impulses at the strike's first four mode frequencies within 15 ms, with amplitudes taken from the contact's penetration history.

**5. Give the latch and stop real geometry and clearances.** Make bevel-tip-on-lip first contact an impact of the bolt's mass at the normal component of edge speed, followed by a LuGre or roughness-driven scrape over the bevel ride (throw/v). Add the deadlatch plunger as its own contact. Let the snap be the bolt shoulder striking the case stop after spring travel. Set leaf-to-stop clearance to 1.6–2.4 mm and strike slop to 0–3.2 mm, both per door instance, and expose slop as the jiggle control. To produce the settle train, give the closed leaf a restoring mode at 25–33 Hz: either silencer stiffness of about 100–430 N/mm at the latch edge, or an explicit first flex mode of the leaf, with the latch-keeper contact as the clipping nonlinearity. Tune stop and keeper restitution so each event drops 2–4 dB (amplitude ratio 0.63–0.79). Add the handle and rose as loose bodies on the spindle with a few degrees of play, running Rath–Rocchesso-style bounces from the leaf's acceleration. Drive hand closes at 0.06–0.1 m/s (gentle), 0.15–0.3 m/s (normal) and 0.3–0.5 m/s (hard) at the strike, so the 40–150 ms precursor spacing falls out of the geometry. Keep faster speeds for wind or a thrown door.

**6. Rebuild the push bar as a box, not a beam.** Model the pad as a pressed-sheet body struck by a soft palm contact (millisecond-scale). Model the rail and centre case as a thin-walled shell with dense modes (n ≈ 0.03 modes/Hz for the assumed ~0.2 m² of 2 mm aluminium, thin-panel η). Model the air inside as delay-line resonators tuned to the case's 845/2450/2860 Hz axial modes and to the rail's length series from about 214 Hz. Couple everything at the mounting points into the steel skin's dense field. The mechanism needs pad bottoming (with or without bumpers 0.1 in before end), crank and drive-bar end-stops, the latch dragging out of the keeper under closer and silencer preload, and on release the spring-return snap of pad, drive bar and latch to full 19 mm throw. Run the push stroke over 50–150 ms. Each end-stop impact should go through steps 1, 3 and 4.

**7. Run the closer in two zones.** Set the sweep to 0.3–0.45 m/s at the edge and the latch zone to 0.05–0.08 m/s over the last 10–15°, so a 19 mm exit latch scrapes for 0.25–0.4 s and snaps 20–50 ms before the silencers. A mis-set latch valve raises the zone speed toward sweep speed and produces the commercial slam. Add hydraulic hiss proportional to valve flow, and backcheck from about 70° of opening.

**8. Calibrate level through the physics and judge through the room.** Scale radiated pressure from contact impulse, with no per-event gain. Then check the result against 1 m A-weighted Fast targets: 60–70 dB gentle, 70–82 dB normal, 85–98 dB slam, 73–79 dB push-bar actuation. Check that the latch events of a light close come within a few dB of, or above, the leaf hit. Check that slam minus normal is +11 to +14 dB. Remember that Lpeak runs 8–14 dB above LAFmax. For every listening round, render through the game's room path and also compare the dry first 50 ms with the recordings' first 50 ms, so the room is not mistaken for the model.

The acceptance tests come straight from the recordings and from the measurements already in hand:

## Conclusion

The verdict that the doors sound synthetic is a counting problem before it is a modelling problem. A thin steel skin carries one bending mode every 1.7 Hz and a timber leaf one every 38 Hz. A render with tens of modes cannot reach the spectral flatness of a real hit, whatever its damping or tilt. Its parts fuse in time because the mechanism has no clearances and no audible bevel impact, and it settles at once because nothing in it can oscillate against the keeper. None of the missing properties requires a recording. They come from modal density computed from the geometry, loss factors per body rather than one Rayleigh curve, contact resolved at audio rate with acceleration noise, and millimetre-scale clearances that turn edge speed into timing. The same analysis explains why noise was right for the car door and wrong for the house door: the regime is set by modal overlap, not by the event type.

Three unknowns limit how precisely this can be done. Building-door edge speeds have never been published; our precursor spacing implies 0.15–0.35 m/s at the strike even on hard closes, far below car doors. Silencer and leaf stiffness have to be inferred from the 30–40 ms settle period. And no source isolates a steel fire-door slam level. The quickest way to close all three would be one instrumented recording session, with an accelerometer on the latch stile and a calibrated meter at 1 m. Until then, the flatness, precursor and settle-train tests above give each change a measurable target that the ear can then confirm or reject.
